Step 1

Major accident hazards

List the hazards this study is protecting against. Take them from the safety case, HAZOP, bow tie or risk register rather than inventing new ones.

Tasks in Step 2 are linked back to these hazards.
Step 2

Safety critical tasks

A task is safety critical if doing it badly could start, worsen or fail to stop one of the hazards above. Include checking, communication and decision tasks, not only hands-on operations.

Priority = consequence + reliance on the person + how often the task is done.
Step 3

Task evidence

Record how you found out what really happens. A study built only on reading procedures will miss the workarounds that matter.

Step 4

Task breakdown

Break each task into the steps a person actually carries out. Use sub-steps where a step has parts worth analysing separately.

Tab through the text boxes; changes save as you type.
Step 5

Human failures and influencing factors

For each step, ask what could go wrong with it. Use the prompts to stay systematic, then note the conditions that make the failure more likely.

Step 6

Safety measures

Work down the list in order. Removing the hazard or the need for the person beats writing another procedure. If most of your measures sit at level 5, the study is not finished.

Step 7

Review

Check the study against the marks of a sound analysis before you issue it.

    How to use this workbench

    The seven steps follow the standard approach to human factors safety critical task analysis used across the energy industry.

    The method in short

    1. Major hazards. Start from the hazards already identified for the facility. If a task has no route to one of them, it does not belong in this study.

    2. Critical tasks. Look for tasks that open the system up, that maintain a protective system and could leave it disabled, or that create the very condition a protective system guards against. Include checks, handovers and decisions.

    3. Evidence. Read the procedure, then watch the task and talk to the people who do it. Differences between the two are usually where the risk lives.

    4. Breakdown. Set out the steps in order. Two levels is usually enough.

    5. Failures and influencing factors. Apply the prompts to each step, then record the workplace, equipment, procedural, organisational and personal conditions that make each failure more likely.

    6. Safety measures. Choose controls from the top of the hierarchy down, and check the control actually suits the type of failure.

    7. Review. Benchmark the study, close the gaps, and put the actions into your normal tracking system.

    Matching the measure to the failure

    The type of failure decides what will work:

    Slip — the right intention, the wrong action. Better labelling, layout and fewer distractions help. More training rarely does.

    Lapse — a step forgotten. Checklists, prompts and place-keeping help.

    Mistake — the action was carried out as intended, but the intention was wrong. Procedures, decision aids, training and competence help.

    Violation — a deliberate departure from the rule. Look at why: supervision, workload, whether the rule is workable, and whether the correct method is the easiest one.

    Hierarchy of measures

    1 Remove the hazard · 2 Remove the human contribution · 3 Prevent or limit the consequence with an engineered barrier · 4 Assure performance mechanically or electrically, for example an interlock · 5 Improve the conditions the person works under.
